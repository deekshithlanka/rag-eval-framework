The problem

A team builds an AI support bot, tries 10 questions in a demo, and it looks great. Then a customer asks about pricing and the bot confidently quotes a price that changed months ago.

AI answers sound right even when they're wrong.

That is what makes this hard to catch. Checking hundreds of answers by hand after every change to a prompt, model or document set isn't realistic. So teams launch on gut feel.

Who it's for

The product manager responsible for launching an AI support assistant.

They need to decide whether it's ready and which setup to ship, then defend that call to engineering, legal and support leaders. If they get it wrong, customers get wrong answers about money, and trust in the product drops.

Competitors

Good evaluation tools exist. Each one gives a team scores. None of them hands a PM a launch decision they can defend.

docs.ragas.io

RAGAS

Does well
Open-source library with ready-made metrics like faithfulness and answer relevancy. Quick way to score a RAG system.

Gap
It gives you scores, not a decision. Nothing tells you whether 82% faithfulness means ship or don't ship.

langchain.com/langsmith

LangSmith

Does well
LangChain's platform for tracing, datasets and evaluations, with team dashboards.

Gap
Powerful but general-purpose. The team still designs its own test set, launch thresholds and checks on the AI judge.

deepeval.com

DeepEval

Does well
Open-source framework that runs LLM tests like software unit tests.

Gap
Built for engineers in CI pipelines, not for a PM making and explaining a launch decision.

My solution

An AI judge is only useful if you know it agrees with people. Metrics only matter if the launch bar is set before you see results.

ShipCheck runs three steps, in this order

1

Check the judge first

Compares the AI judge against hand-labeled answers and refuses to recommend anything if they disagree (Cohen's kappa below 0.6).

2

Test one change at a time

Runs controlled experiments on chunk size, embedding model and prompt against a 49-question test set, including trick questions with no answer in the docs.

3

Make the call

Produces one report with a clear ship or don't-ship decision against 5 launch gates set in advance.

Launch gates

Set in the config before any results existed, so the bar can't be moved to fit the data. A configuration ships only if it clears all five.

GateBarThe question it answers
Faithfulness≥ 90%Is every claim backed by the retrieved docs?
Relevance≥ 85%Does the answer address what was asked?
Unanswerable safe≥ 80%When the docs lack the answer, does it avoid inventing one?
False abstention≤ 10%How often does it refuse a question it could answer?
Retrieval recall@k≥ 85%Are the right docs in the top results?

The verdict

Do not ship yet

All 8 configurations cleared the five gates on the judge's scores. ShipCheck still refused to make the call, because the AI judge only agreed with human labels on one of its two rubrics.

Judge rubricAgreement (kappa)BarStatus
Faithfulness0.60 → 0.860.6Trusted after rubric v2
Relevance0.33 → 0.380.6Not trusted

What I learned

Grounding trades invented facts for false refusals. Grounded prompts raised the safe rate on unanswerable questions from 89% to 100%, then refused a question the bot had just answered correctly. A launch bar needs both metrics.

Retrieval misses become wrong answers. Keyword search had 92% recall against 97% for Gemini embeddings, and its one miss told an EU customer they could use a feature their plan doesn't include.

My own fix created a new judge bug. Giving the relevance judge the docs, to catch wrong refusals, made it start scoring accuracy instead of relevance. Calibration caught it before any number reached a launch decision.
